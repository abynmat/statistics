# 9. The frontier, 2023-2026

> Previous: [8. Sensitivity and validation](08-sensitivity-and-validation.md) · Next: [10. Tooling and cheat sheet](10-tooling-and-cheatsheet.md)

This chapter is a map of active research, not a set of settled methods. Where a result is a preprint, it is labelled as such. Expect parts of this chapter to age fastest. Nothing here has a runnable example: the point is to know what exists, what problem it targets, and what it still assumes.

## 9.1 Proximal causal inference

**Problem.** Ignorability given $X$ fails because $X$ is only a noisy proxy of the true confounder $U$.

**Idea.** Find two kinds of proxy. A *treatment-side* proxy $Z$ (a negative-control exposure) is related to $U$ but does not affect $Y$ directly. An *outcome-side* proxy $W$ (a negative-control outcome) is related to $U$ but is not affected by $T$. Under completeness conditions, the pair identifies the causal effect through a **confounding bridge function** $h$ that solves the integral equation $E[Y \mid Z, T, X] = E[h(W, T, X) \mid Z, T, X]$. This generalises the negative-control idea from "detect bias" to "remove bias" ([Tchetgen Tchetgen et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.10982v1.pdf); [confounding bridge, Miao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/1808.04945v4)).

**Recent developments.** Regression-based two-stage estimators that are easy to run in standard software ([arXiv 2402.00335](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00335v2)); proximal learners for heterogeneous effects, such as the P-learner ([arXiv 2301.10913](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.10913v1)); robustness to many invalid proxies ([arXiv 2506.13152](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13152v1)); bias formulas for when the proximal assumptions themselves fail ([arXiv 2208.00105](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.00105)); and accessible overviews ([*Demystifying proximal causal inference*, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.24413v1)).

**What it still assumes.** Valid proxies, which are untestable, plus completeness conditions that are hard to verify. It swaps one untestable assumption for a different, sometimes more defensible, one.

## 9.2 Transportability and data fusion

**Problem.** Your experiment ran in population A (a pilot city, a trial cohort). You need the effect in population B.

**Idea.** Add **selection nodes** to the DAG that mark where the two populations' mechanisms differ. do-calculus then tells you whether, and how, the effect in B can be computed from experimental data in A plus observational data in B. Bareinboim and Pearl give complete algorithms for transportability and for the broader **data-fusion** problem of combining heterogeneous datasets collected under different conditions ([Bareinboim & Pearl, PNAS 2016](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1510507113); [completeness results](https://ftp.cs.ucla.edu/pub/stat_ser/r390-L.pdf)). The econometrics literature has been absorbing these tools ([Hünermund & Bareinboim](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09104v3)).

**Why it is frontier.** Theory is ahead of practice. The applied counterparts (generalisability weighting, trial-to-target transport estimators with doubly robust scores, combining RCTs with observational data to correct confounding) are an active area in biostatistics and tech experimentation.

## 9.3 Causal representation learning

**Problem.** Causal inference assumes the variables are given: age, dose, price. In images, video, text or sensor streams, the causal variables are latent, and pixels are not causes of each other.

**Idea.** Learn low-dimensional latent variables that behave like the variables of an SCM, so that interventions, distribution shifts and multiple environments act on a few of them in a sparse, modular way ([Schölkopf et al., *Toward causal representation learning*, 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2102.11107)). Identifiability results show when such latents can be recovered up to simple transformations: with interventional data, multiple environments, temporal structure, or paired observations. Recent work connects CRL to the **invariance principle** ([arXiv 2409.02772](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.02772v2)) and to understanding **foundation models**, recovering human-interpretable concepts from model representations ([arXiv 2402.09236](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.09236v2)).

**What it still assumes.** Strong structural assumptions about how latents generate observations, and usually some form of intervention or environment variation. CRL is mostly theory and synthetic benchmarks so far, with real applications in robotics, biology (perturbation screens) and interpretability.

## 9.4 Foundation models *for* causal inference

**Problem.** Every causal analysis today is bespoke: choose nuisance models, tune, cross-fit, check.

**Idea.** Borrow from **prior-data fitted networks** (PFNs, such as TabPFN): pretrain a transformer on millions of *synthetic* datasets drawn from a prior over SCMs, where the true effects are known, so that at test time it maps a new dataset to effect estimates in one forward pass (in-context learning). Three 2025 preprints follow this recipe:

* **CausalPFN.** Amortised CATE/ATE estimation with calibrated uncertainty, no task-specific tuning ([arXiv 2506.07918](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07918v1)).
* **Do-PFN.** Predicts interventional outcomes from observational data without being given the causal graph ([arXiv 2506.06039](http://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06039v1)).
* **CausalFM.** A general framework for training PFN-style causal foundation models across several causal inference settings ([arXiv 2506.10914](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10914v1)). Follow-ups extend this to continuous treatments ([arXiv 2605.15133](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15133v1)).

**Caution.** Identification does not disappear. A PFN can only be as right as its training prior. If your real data violate the prior's assumptions, for example by having unobserved confounding the prior never simulated, the model will give a confident answer to the wrong question. The open questions are which priors make the learned estimators trustworthy, and how to diagnose when a dataset lies outside the prior.

## 9.5 Causal reasoning *in* LLMs, and how to evaluate it

**Benchmarks.** **CLadder** (NeurIPS 2023) generates questions from known causal graphs on all three rungs of Pearl's ladder, computes ground-truth answers with a causal inference engine, and phrases them in natural language. The task was hard for the LLMs of the time, and the authors' structured CausalCoT prompting helped ([Jin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.04350v2); [code](https://github.com/causalNLP/cladder)). Other benchmarks test whether models update correctly after an intervention on a described data-generating process ([arXiv 2404.05545](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05545v1)).

**Skepticism about benchmarks.** A critical review argues that many causal benchmarks can be solved by retrieving domain knowledge rather than by causal reasoning ([arXiv 2407.08029](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08029v1)). "Causal parrots" argues that models recite causal facts found in their training text ([arXiv 2308.13067](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.13067)). A 2026 preprint finds that on anti-commonsense CLadder items, a linear probe on hidden states recovers the evidence-supported answer much more often than the model's spoken yes/no does. A wrong answer may reflect a failure to *express* what the model encodes, not a failure to encode it ([arXiv 2605.25891](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25891v1)). Questions about how procedurally generated benchmarks can mislead are themselves now a topic ([arXiv 2610.38100](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.38100)).

**Takeaway.** Benchmark accuracy on causal questions is weak evidence of causal *reasoning*. Prefer tasks where the answer contradicts commonsense or depends on numbers given only in the prompt, and analyse failure modes rather than one accuracy number.

## 9.6 Causal inference *for* LLM evaluation and development

The reverse direction may matter more in practice. LLM development is full of causal questions: does this prompt change *cause* better answers? Does this training-data mix *cause* the capability gain? Should a query be routed to a bigger model?

* **Counterfactual prompting as an experiment.** Editing a prompt to change one attribute (a name, a dialect, a detail) is a treatment. But each edit also changes incidental wording, so it is a *compound* treatment, which violates the "no hidden versions of treatment" part of SUTVA. Better baselines and metrics are needed to isolate the attribute's effect ([arXiv 2605.01048](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01048v1)).
* **Estimating effects of text interventions** by using LLMs to produce the counterfactual texts needed to define and estimate the effect of changing one attribute of a document ([arXiv 2410.21474](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.21474v1)).
* **LLMs as causal models of their own outputs.** Methods for generating *counterfactual outputs* of a stochastic LLM (what it would have said under prompt $x^*$, holding its sampling noise fixed) treat the model as a structural causal model and enable rung-three questions about model behaviour ([arXiv 2509.22297](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22297)).
* **Interpretability claims as causal claims.** Mechanistic interpretability already uses interventions such as activation patching. A 2026 position paper argues that causal inference supplies the criteria for when such claims generalise ([arXiv 2602.16698](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16698)).
* **A broader agenda** argues that causal methods are under-used in LLM development and evaluation, for example in routing, data attribution and A/B tests of model changes ([arXiv 2605.25998](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.25998v1.pdf)). Classical tools from this guide, including randomisation, interference-aware designs, CUPED and sensitivity analysis, apply directly to evaluation pipelines.

## 9.7 Other active threads

* **DiD beyond binary staggered treatment:** continuous treatments ([Callaway, Goodman-Bacon & Sant'Anna](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.02637v7.pdf)), multiple confounding events ([arXiv 2409.05184](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.05184v1)), cohort-specific HonestDiD ([arXiv 2509.01829](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01829)).
* **Synthetic control theory:** large-sample properties under selection on unobservables ([arXiv 2311.13575](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.13575v2)), and identification and inference with confounding ([arXiv 2312.00955](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.00955)).
* **Variance reduction in experiments** beyond CUPED: in-experiment covariates, post-stratification for heavy-tailed metrics ([arXiv 2606.04110](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04110)), and multivariate adjustment for switchbacks ([arXiv 2608.24038](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24038v1)).
* **Distributional and joint counterfactuals:** beyond average effects to the joint distribution of $(Y(0), Y(1))$ ([arXiv 2601.20405](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20405v2)).
* **Conformal causal inference:** distribution-free prediction intervals for individual treatment effects (Lei & Candès, 2021, and successors).
* **Causal ML with LLM features:** text as treatment, outcome or confounder, using embeddings as adjustment variables. This raises its own positivity and measurement-error issues.

## 9.8 How to keep up

* Conferences: **CLeaR** (Causal Learning and Reasoning), UAI, NeurIPS and ICML causality workshops, the ACIC data challenge, and the Online Causal Inference Seminar.
* Econometrics and statistics: *Journal of Econometrics*, *Econometrica*, *JASA*, *JRSS-B*, and NBER working papers.
* Code ecosystems: py-why (DoWhy, EconML, causal-learn), `grf`, `did`, `synthdid`, `HonestDiD`.

## 9.9 Key takeaways

* **Proximal inference** and **transportability** extend *identification*. They are the most conceptually important advances for practitioners.
* **Causal representation learning** asks where causal variables come from, which is essential for perception and interpretability.
* **Causal foundation models** amortise estimation but inherit their training prior's assumptions.
* **LLMs and causality** run in both directions. LLM causal-reasoning benchmarks are fragile. Causal methods for LLM evaluation are under-used and immediately practical.

## Appendix B: open problems worth knowing about

1. **Validating identification assumptions.** Every frontier method rests on some untestable assumption: valid proxies, a correct selection diagram, a correct prior. Practical diagnostics for these are scarce.
2. **Uncertainty for amortised estimators.** When a PFN outputs an effect, what does its uncertainty mean when the data lie outside the training prior?
3. **Measurement and text.** When treatments, outcomes or confounders are measured with an LLM or classifier, measurement error flows into causal estimates. Corrections exist but are not routine.
4. **Interference at scale.** Platform experiments almost always have some interference. Designs that are both cheap and unbiased for global effects remain an active area.
5. **Benchmarks that cannot be gamed.** Varsortability in discovery and memorisation in LLM causal benchmarks show how easily benchmarks leak information. Better benchmark design is itself a research problem.
